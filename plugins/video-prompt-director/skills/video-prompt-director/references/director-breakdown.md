## 1. Why it comes before the storyboard

A storyboard artist who is handed only a script has to invent the direction: what the scene is for, where people stand, how they move, what the camera favours and why each cut happens. Those choices then arrive mixed into panel drawings and prompt wording, where nobody reviews them as choices. The breakdown makes them explicit and approvable first. It is written from the approved screenplay and the Cinematic Bible (`references/cinematic-bible.md`).

## 2. Per scene

| Field | The decision |
|---|---|
| Objective | What the scene must achieve in the film, in one sentence |
| Wants | What each character wants in this scene (an act, not a mood) |
| Audience feels | The intended response, and how it shifts |
| Learns / kept from them | What information the audience gets, and what stays hidden |
| Progression | The emotional beats in order (the turns) |
| Key image | The single most important visual moment, and how it is shown |
| Room | Landmarks in order along the scene's axis (section 3), the established camera side, the 180° line |
| Blocking | Where each person starts, their moves, where they end (section 4) |
| Props | The objects that matter, with their state |
| Camera strategy | Sizes and lenses for each kind of beat, where the one move (if any) goes |
| Lighting strategy | The location line from the bible plus anything the story changes |
| Editing intention | What the cuts are for: whose reaction, which insert, where to hold |
| Exit | How the scene hands over to the next one (cut on action, time cut, sound) |

## 3. The room as landmarks

Describe each room as an ordered list of landmarks along the axis seen from the established camera side (door → table → sink → window), with rough depth when it matters, never invented measurements. People stand at landmarks or "between A and B". Screen left and right are then derived from positions and the camera side; they are never a property of a person. A camera that crosses to the far side flips left and right, and the plan must say how that crossing is shown.

## 4. Blocking

For each person: start landmark, moves in order, end landmark. Movement is the actors'; only a camera that changes side crosses the line. If someone must be somewhere at the start of a scene that they weren't at the end of the last one, the breakdown says how (time cut, off-screen move, a shown move).

## 5. Starting state of props and people

List every object and appearance the story depends on, with its state at the start of the film and who it concerns (the worn gloves by the sink; the bike on the back porch with the cracked helmet on the handlebar). Every later change must be caused on screen, off screen with its result shown, or by a time cut. This list is the seed of the continuity ledger (`references/continuity.md` section 7).

## 6. What the storyboard then may and may not decide

The storyboard decides framing details, panel count and the panel-to-generation mapping (`references/storyboard.md`). It does not decide, on its own, the scene's objective, who stands where, the screen sides, the props' states or what each cut is for. If drawing a panel shows that the breakdown can't work (an impossible eyeline, a move that can't fit the clip), revise the breakdown and say so; don't quietly redirect the scene in the storyboard.

## 7. Checks before approval

- Every screenplay scene has a breakdown, and every breakdown scene exists in the screenplay.
- Everyone in the scene's cast is blocked; every start and end is a landmark of that room.
- The 180° line runs between two blocked people (or two landmarks).
- Every prop the screenplay uses has a starting state.
- Each scene's key image can be shown with the chosen camera and blocking.
