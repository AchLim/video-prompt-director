# Episode beats: hook, context, escalation, cliffhanger

Load whenever the source is a short drama or a vertical series episode, before you cut it into panels and again before you hand back prompts. A story can be good on the page and fail on screen because the panels spread it out evenly. Viewers decide in seconds, arrive at a random episode and often watch with the sound off. The panels have to carry the hook, the context, the escalation and the ending in pictures.

## 1. What goes wrong when a good script becomes clips

| Failure on screen | What caused it |
|---|---|
| Nobody knows who these people are | Relationships and status were only in dialogue, or in a script note that never reached a panel |
| The opening is flat | Panel 1 is an establishing shot, a generic humiliation, or a line spoken before the viewer cares |
| The episode feels like one note | Every clip repeats the same emotion in the same room with the same framing |
| The twist does nothing | The evidence is blurred, illegible or never shown large, so the viewer cannot read what the characters read |
| The ending is a pause | The last clip is a reaction or a spoken deflection after the tension is gone |
| It feels choppy | Cuts every few seconds with changing lenses and angles, and nothing joining one clip to the next |

## 2. The beat map: plan it before the panels

Write five lines before the panel list. If one cannot be filled, ask the user or flag the gap.

```
Hook image (first 2-3 s):
Cold-viewer line (who, relation, wrong or danger, what happens next):
Escalation: what is worse at each clip (one line per clip):
Evidence the viewer must read, and the panel that shows it large:
Ending image (the last frame) and the one question it leaves:
```

Also name one detail that is planted early and changes meaning at the end (an object, a mark, a phrase). Show it clearly in an early panel and frame it the same way when it returns.

## 3. Hook panel

- The first panel starts inside a problem, with a person and something wrong in frame in the first 2 to 3 seconds. It is a close or medium shot of a face, a hand, an object or evidence, not a wide of the location.
- The hook must be readable with the sound off: what changed, who is affected, what the person can do now.
- Choose a concrete cause for attention: a consequence shown before its cause (the signed paper already on the table), an interrupted plan, impossible evidence, a relationship changing in front of others, a costly offer, or a broken rule. A generic humiliation (liquid thrown, a slap, a forced kneel) is a stock opener; use it only with a fresh specific angle, and not as the whole episode.
- Do not hold the first spoken line back for an entry beat on a hook panel. Start on the action (`references/pacing.md` section 3).
- The hook must be paid by the episode. Do not open on something the episode never explains or reinterprets.

## 4. Context: the cold-viewer test

By about second 5 to 8, a stranger watching with no sound must be able to answer four questions:

1. Who is the lead, and who are they facing?
2. What is the relationship (spouses, boss and employee, debtor and creditor)?
3. Who has the power right now, and is that about to move?
4. What is at stake?

Show it with things the camera sees: uniform, vehicle, tool, ring, seating, who stands, a name card, a company sign. When a relationship or status cannot be shown quickly, plan a short caption or label for the edit, and say so in the panel. Do not rely on a spoken explanation, and do not open on a narrator.

Dramatic irony is the fastest context. If the audience can see something the other characters miss (an object that shows the humble man's real rank), every later insult turns into anticipation.

Test: write one sentence for each of the four questions using only panels 1 to 2 with the sound off. If you cannot, add the visible marker or reorder the panels before writing a prompt.

## 5. Escalation

Every clip must make things worse, tighter or costlier than the clip before. Ways to escalate without more people or more action:

- Remove an easy exit (the door locks, the call drops, the card is declined).
- Add a witness or an arrival.
- Raise the price of the choice.
- Shorten the clock.
- Turn the evidence against the lead.
- Show the other side already moving.

A run of clips that repeats one emotion (humiliation, anger, grief) at the same intensity is flat even when each is well made. If three adjacent panels show the same emotion, one of them is replaced by a new fact, a new pressure or a change of power. Change the framing with the escalation: tighter on the turn, wider when the room joins in.

## 6. Story-critical evidence must be readable

A signature, a message, a document, a photo or a screen that the plot turns on is the most important picture in the episode. Do not leave it as an illegible smear that someone will fix later.

- Give it its own insert panel, held long enough to read, large in frame.
- Models render legible text unreliably (`references/constraints.md`), so plan the readable text as an overlay added in the edit, and write in the panel what the overlay says and when it appears. The generated shot supplies the paper, the hand and the held frame; the edit supplies the words.
- Keep the generated shot free of garbled text so the overlay sits on a clean surface.
- Cut from the evidence to the face that reads it. The reaction tells the viewer what it means.

## 7. Cliffhanger panel

The last panel changes the situation before the cut. It does not just withhold an answer.

- End on a visible change: a decision, a discovery, a threat arriving, a line crossed, a door opening. Not on a spoken deflection or a reaction held until the tension drains.
- Use the early detail from the beat map: the object or mark returns and now means something else.
- Cut on the inhale: the instant before the answer, as a clear visual state the next episode can start from (`references/storyboard.md` section 3, "Ends on").
- Leave one specific question, such as whose name is that, or what did she just switch on.
- A quiet ending is allowed when the lead makes a choice they must live with. Even then the last frame shows something changed.
- Do not add a closing reaction clip after the change. Do not end on "to be continued" imagery with no tie to the episode.

## 8. Checks before approval

- Panel 1 has a person and a problem in the first 2 to 3 seconds and works with the sound off.
- The cold-viewer test passes from panels 1 to 2.
- Each panel makes something worse, tighter or costlier than the one before.
- The evidence the plot turns on has its own insert, readable, with its overlay planned.
- The planted detail appears early and again at the end, framed the same way.
- The last panel shows a change, not a pause, and leaves one specific question.
- If any of these fail, revise the panels, not the prompts.
